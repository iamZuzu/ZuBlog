## Overview

*Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011) is Daniel Kahneman's summary of a lifetime of research, much of it done with his long-time collaborator Amos Tversky. Kahneman was a psychologist who won the Nobel Prize in economics in 2002, mostly for prospect theory: the work that showed people don't weigh gains and losses the way the textbooks assumed.

The book makes one big claim. Your mind runs on two modes of thinking, and most of your errors come from the fast one taking over jobs it isn't fit for. The rest of the book is evidence, experiments and consequences.

It's not a light read. It runs to roughly 500 pages, and some chapters feel like a lecture. But few books change how you see your own decisions this much.

---

## The Core Idea: Two Systems

**System 1** is fast, automatic and effortless. It reads faces, finishes "bread and...", flinches at a loud noise and gives you a gut answer before you've asked for one.

**System 2** is slow, deliberate and effortful. It does long division, checks an argument and fills in a tax form. It's also lazy, and it signs off on whatever System 1 hands it unless something forces a closer look.

Kahneman is careful to say these are characters in a story, not regions of the brain. The story is useful anyway. Most of the time System 1 is brilliant and we'd be paralysed without it. The trouble is that it's confident even when it's wrong, and System 2 rarely checks.

The classic test:

> A bat and a ball cost $1.10 in total. The bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?

The answer that jumps out is 10 cents. It's wrong: the ball costs 5 cents. Plenty of very bright students get it wrong, not because they can't do the maths but because the fast answer feels right and nobody checks it.

## The Ideas That Stuck With Me

### **1. What You See Is All There Is**

Kahneman calls this WYSIATI. System 1 builds the most coherent story it can from the information in front of it, and never asks what's missing. A tidy story built on three facts feels more convincing than a messy one built on thirty.

**Why it matters:** this is the root of overconfidence. Our confidence tracks how *coherent* the story is, not how *complete* the evidence is.

**Application:** before a decision, ask "what would I need to know that I don't know?" and make that list longer than feels comfortable.

---

### **2. Anchoring**

Any number you see before an estimate pulls the estimate toward it, even when the number is obviously irrelevant. In one experiment, people asked whether Gandhi was older or younger than 114 when he died then gave much higher estimates of his age at death than people first asked about 35.

**Why it matters:** the first number on the table, whether an asking price, a budget, last year's risk rating or a salary offer, shapes everything that follows.

**Application:** form your own estimate *before* you see anyone else's number. In negotiations, be the one to set the anchor, or say plainly that you're ignoring theirs.

---

### **3. Availability**

We judge how likely something is by how easily examples come to mind. Plane crashes feel more dangerous than car journeys because they're vivid and get reported. Recent and dramatic events crowd out base rates.

**Application:** when something "feels" common or risky, look up the actual frequency. Your memory is a biased sample.

---

### **4. Substitution**

When System 1 faces a hard question, it quietly answers an easier one instead. "Is this a good investment?" becomes "Do I like this company?" "How happy am I with my life?" becomes "What mood am I in right now?"

The famous example is the Linda problem. Linda is described as outspoken and concerned with social justice. Most people rate "Linda is a bank teller and active in the feminist movement" as *more* likely than "Linda is a bank teller", which is logically impossible. They've answered "who does she resemble?" instead of "what's most probable?"

**Application:** when you reach a quick answer to a hard question, check which question you actually answered.

---

### **5. Prospect Theory and Loss Aversion**

This is the heart of the book and the work behind Kahneman's Nobel. Three findings:

- **We judge outcomes against a reference point**, not by total wealth. A $1,000 bonus feels great if you expected nothing and awful if you expected $5,000.
- **Losses hurt roughly twice as much as equal gains feel good.** That's why people hold losing shares too long and sell winners too early.
- **Framing changes choices.** People are more willing to accept a surgery described as having a 90% survival rate than the identical surgery described as having a 10% mortality rate.

**Why it matters:** much of bad investing behaviour is loss aversion in disguise. Morgan Housel's [The Psychology of Money](/posts/psychology-of-money) is, in many ways, a practical guide to living with this chapter.

**Application:** decide your rules for selling before you buy. Look at decisions as a whole portfolio, not one painful loss at a time.

---

### **6. The Planning Fallacy**

We plan using the best case for *our* project and ignore how similar projects actually went. Kahneman admits to this himself: a curriculum project his team expected to finish in about two years took eight, and the evidence that it would was available from the start.

**Application:** use the *outside view*. Ask how long and how much projects like this one usually take, start from that base rate, and only then adjust for what's special about yours. Kahneman also recommends a *pre-mortem*: imagine it's a year from now and the project has failed, then write down why.

---

### **7. Regression to the Mean**

Extreme results tend to be followed by more ordinary ones, purely by chance. Kahneman describes Israeli flight instructors who were convinced that praise made cadets worse and criticism made them better. In reality, a great landing is usually followed by a worse one and a bad landing by a better one, whatever the instructor says.

**Application:** don't build stories around one exceptional quarter, a hot fund manager or a terrible week. Wait for more data.

---

### **8. The Two Selves**

The *experiencing self* lives through each moment. The *remembering self* keeps score afterwards, and it judges an experience mainly by its most intense moment and how it ended, largely ignoring how long it lasted.

**Why it matters:** we make future choices based on memories, not experiences. A great holiday with a bad last day can be remembered as a bad holiday.

**Application:** endings matter. Finish meetings, projects and trips well, because that's what people will remember.

## What Has Aged Badly

This needs saying plainly. Since the book came out, psychology has gone through a replication crisis: many well-known studies failed when other researchers repeated them. Several studies cited in the book's chapter on *priming* (the idea that tiny cues, like reading words about old age, change behaviour, like walking more slowly) have not held up.

To his credit, Kahneman acknowledged this publicly, admitting he had placed too much faith in small, underpowered studies. Some critics go further and argue that a lot of the book rests on weaker evidence than its confident tone suggests.

My take: read the priming chapter with a large pinch of salt. The core ideas, including System 1 and System 2, anchoring, loss aversion, the planning fallacy and regression to the mean, rest on much stronger and more repeated evidence. Still, it's a little ironic that a book about overconfidence was itself overconfident in places.

## My Verdict

**Read it if** you make decisions under uncertainty for a living: investing, managing projects, assessing risk, hiring or negotiating. It gives you a vocabulary for mistakes you already make, and naming a bias is the first step to catching it.

**Skip or skim it if** you want a quick, practical read. It's long, dense in places, and repeats itself. The first two parts and the part on prospect theory give you most of the value.

**The honest limitation:** Kahneman himself says that knowing about biases barely helps you avoid them in the moment. System 1 doesn't read books. The real payoff is in *processes*: checklists, base rates, pre-mortems and asking someone else to challenge your thinking. It's far easier to spot other people's mistakes than your own, so use that.

**Rating:** 4.5 / 5. It's one of the most important books on how we think, and it's slightly marked down for its length and for the chapters that haven't survived scrutiny.

## How to Apply It

**Before any big decision**
- Write down your own estimate before you look at anyone else's numbers
- Ask what information is missing, not just what's in front of you
- Look up the base rate: how often does this kind of thing actually work out?

**For plans and projects**
- Use the outside view: start from how long and how much similar projects took
- Run a pre-mortem: assume it failed, and list the reasons why

**For money**
- Set your rules for selling before you buy
- Judge your portfolio as a whole, not one loss at a time
- Be suspicious of a single brilliant result, whether it's yours or a fund manager's

**For yourself**
- When an answer arrives instantly to a hard question, slow down and check it
- Ask someone you trust to poke holes in your reasoning, because spotting other people's errors is easier
